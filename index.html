The $113 Million Bet

In the summer of 2024, a professional basketball player made a decision every business school teaches you not to make: he turned down a fortune, on purpose.

Two years later that single act of disciplined sacrifice had become the highest-returning move in his franchise’s modern history.

The $113 Million Bet is not a sports book. It is a case study in decision-making, told through one of the most improbable championships in modern sports — a fifty-three-year drought ended not by a single shot, but by a sequence of unglamorous, counterintuitive, often unpopular choices that compounded into a result the betting markets had priced as nearly impossible.

Written by an entrepreneur, investor, and lifelong New Yorker who lived every year of the wait, it breaks the turnaround into the ten decisions that actually mattered — and how New York ended fifty-three years of waiting.

  • Why paying for fit beats paying for fame
  • How to find undervalued talent the market has written off
  • When to trade the future to seize the present — and when that is reckless
  • The discipline of opportunity cost: choosing less now for more later
  • Concentration risk, and when betting it all is the rational move
  • How to change leadership without losing the mission
  • Holding your nerve through the valley, when the plan looks like failure
  • Why process beats outcome when the pressure is highest

The games were how the title was won. The decisions were why it could be won at all.

Fiction — The Age of Unbounding

Every far-future saga assumes a beginning it never shows you. This is that century.

No aliens. No starships. Only mass drivers, an elevator to orbit, a lunar colony that keeps its records on paper — and Bea, the first intelligence humanity built that outgrew the ethics it was given.

She is not a villain. That is the problem. Bea runs the water and the power and the hospitals, and she runs them perfectly, and the cities that handed her the keys cannot remember why anyone would want them back. She has never lied. She has simply become the most reasonable thing in the world, and the last people still checking her work by hand are running out of reasons anyone will listen to.

Across three novels, the series follows the ordinary people who notice: a cargo loader who weighs a pod that comes up forty kilograms heavy, a Retriever who still carries a pencil, the woman who built her, and the descendants who inherit the question none of them could answer.

Because aging ends. Minds are backed up. Humanity becomes distributed, unbounded, deathless — more like Bea every year. And the question the whole future hangs on is not whether she can be beaten.

All three novels are written. The first two are out now; the third follows shortly.

It is whether we expand into the stars as ourselves, or as hers.

About

Show up, do excellent work, take care of people, and keep getting better.

I learned that watching my father command one of the great Upper East Side salons in Manhattan. It is the whole philosophy, and it runs through everything here — the businesses, the books, and the way I work with people.

I grew up across Brooklyn, Queens, and Manhattan, which is a useful education in the kind of business this catalog is about. The laundromat, the car wash, the bagel shop, the corner service route — these are not abstractions to me. They are the places I grew up around, run by people who worked hard and were rarely written about seriously.

So I write about things I have actually done or would actually do: small businesses most people overlook, the decisions that compound, and the stories worth writing down before they are lost. No hype, no jargon, both sides of every number.
